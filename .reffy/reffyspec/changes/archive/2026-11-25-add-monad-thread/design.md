- Ch1 §IV moves straight from Spinoza to Kant. It skips the figure who stands between them historically and conceptually, and it leaves their question open with no structure for holding it.

## Goals / Non-Goals
- Goals:
  - Bring in Leibniz once in Ch1 as the link between Spinoza and Kant, then use him in Ch8 to name the participant.
  - Read the assembly as a harmony that is kept, not pre-established, and say so as the same correction the book makes to Plato.
  - Give the book three altitudes (record, participants, frame) that hold the Spinoza–Kant question without settling it. Forecast them in Ch1 and set them out in Ch10.
  - Keep *participant* (persists) and *projection* (derived, disposable) apart.
- Non-Goals:
  - A seventh component. A participant is not an entry, and Ch7 is untouched.
  - A new prediction. The five questions are proposed as a form, not demonstrated. The applied evidence is self-referential: both participants placed so far build the assembly itself. A participant that looks outward has not been placed yet.
  - The term "use case", or any account of the applied work, its use cases or its stack.
  - Leibniz's theodicy, his calculus, the best of all possible worlds, or the Leibniz–Clarke correspondence. Only the monad, windowlessness, mirroring, harmony, the complete concept and the identity of indiscernibles come in.
  - Edits to Ch10 §I or §IV. The three-altitude paragraph in §VI points back to them.
  - A sentence in Ch7 where the assembly's consumers are discussed. The suggestion marks it as "possibly". It is left out to keep Ch7's anatomy untouched (see Open Questions).

## Decisions
- **Decision: The book's word is *participant*. *Monad* is Leibniz's name for it, used where the Leibnizian reading is in play.**
  - Rationale: "use case" is the applied work's word and reads as software jargon. *Party* is already loose in ordinary prose. *Participant* says what the thing does in the loop, which is to take part in it, and leaves *monad* free for the philosophical claim. This mirrors the mode thread, where the unit got one name and its Spinozist sense.

- **Decision: Ch1 §IV gains one paragraph on Leibniz, between the Spinoza paragraph and the Kant paragraph.**
  - Content: Leibniz visited Spinoza in The Hague in 1676 and read parts of the *Ethics* in manuscript. The *Monadology* (1714) is in large part his answer: not one substance but infinitely many simple ones, monads, each a point of view on the whole. Three claims: monads are windowless (§7), so none acts causally on another. Each mirrors the whole universe from its own point of view, and no two mirrors are alike (§56). They agree anyway, through a pre-established harmony (§78), not through interaction. Leibniz needs God to establish the harmony. Plant the monad and the harmony for Chapter 8, and flag the guarantee as the place the book will part from him.
  - The Kant paragraph's opening is adjusted so the wall is a reaction to Leibniz as well. Kant's critical turn reacted largely against the Leibniz–Wolff school, and Kant had built no wall while he was still in it. The Spinoza paragraph is not changed.
  - The Kant paragraph's closing gains ≤ 2 sentences forecasting the three altitudes and pointing to Chapter 10. The existing "It takes something from each side" sentence stays, reworded only as far as the forecast needs.
  - The inheritance requirement for the Spinoza passage still holds: Spinoza and Kant are still the poles of the tension, and Leibniz stands between them without resolving it.

- **Decision: Ch8 §III gains ≤ 2 paragraphs, placed directly after the paragraph that names the mode and before "A unit, though, is not a tolerance." The section's cap rises from five paragraphs to seven.**
  - Rationale: the participant is the counterpart of the unit, so it belongs right after the unit. A separate section would renumber Ch8 again only one draft after the last renumbering.
  - Content the passage must carry:
    1. **The participant.** "If the mode is the loop's unit, the monad is its participant": a purpose that takes part in the loop, recording what happens, giving it meaning, deriving state from it and acting on it. Pointer to Ch1. A participant is not a seventh component.
    2. **The five questions, in prose:** what happens that is worth recording; who records it; what it means; what is derived from it; who acts on it, and where. Answering them states the participant's complete concept, in Leibniz's sense. Once a participant is stated this way, it can join the loop without knowing any other participant.
    3. **Harmony and mirror.** The assembly is the harmony: participants agree because each reads the same declared meaning, not because they call one another. This is the narrow waist seen from the side of the parties it serves. Each participant's derived state is its mirror, the same record seen from one point of view.
    4. **Identity of indiscernibles, as a test.** Two participants that answer all five questions the same way are one participant.
    5. **The correction.** Leibniz's harmony is pre-established, guaranteed by God, which is Plato's flaw again: a form assumed to hold by its own nature. The assembly's harmony is kept, held by enforcement and tested at the gates. Its authority is positional and earned (echoing Ch1).
    6. **Participant, not projection.** The participant, meaning the purpose and its five answers, persists. A projection generated to serve it is derived, plural and disposable (Ch9). Monads are substances, so the monad is the participant and never the projection.
    7. **Sized claim.** The five questions are offered as a proposed form, not a demonstrated one.

- **Decision: Ch10 §VI gains one paragraph on the three altitudes. It goes after the retention-era paragraph and before Langer's paragraph.**
  - Content:
    - **The record (Spinoza):** one order under two descriptions, with the mode as its unit.
    - **The participants (Leibniz):** many points of view, coordinated by a harmony that is kept.
    - **The frame (Kant, the later Wittgenstein, Langer):** the conditions under which anything can appear in the assembly at all, and the judgment that draws and amends them.
    - The five questions belong to the frame altitude, because they are the form any participant must take in order to appear. Kant's *Critique of Judgment* names the faculty that fills them, bringing particulars under concepts. The person who rules on what an act was is exercising it, and this is §IV's "no frame draws itself".
    - The paragraph says plainly that Spinoza and Leibniz disagreed about exactly this (one substance or many), and that the book uses each at a different altitude rather than settling their dispute. It does not claim Spinoza and Kant are reconciled. It claims only that the assembly needs both, at different heights: an immanent record below and a frame drawn from outside above.
  - Rationale: the frame altitude ends with Langer and Wittgenstein, which hands on naturally to Langer's last paragraph. The suggestion says "close on them". Here that means close the argument on them, with Langer still closing the book.

- **Decision: Summaries.** Each summary is kept in three places, and all copies are edited together so they stay identical:
  - Ch1 (`index.md`, `part-1.md`, front-matter `description`): add Leibniz between Spinoza and Kant.
  - Ch8 (`index.md`, `part-3.md`, front-matter `description`): add the participant and the assembly as its kept harmony.
  - Ch10 (`index.md`, `part-4.md`, front-matter `description`): add the three altitudes.

- **Decision: Works Cited.** Add Leibniz's *Monadology* (recommended: in *Philosophical Essays*, ed. and trans. Roger Ariew and Daniel Garber, Hackett, 1989). Add Kant's *Critique of Judgment* (recommended: trans. Werner S. Pluhar, Hackett, 1987). The book uses the traditional title. The Cambridge edition (Guyer and Matthews, 2000), which would match the *Critique of Pure Reason* entry, is titled *Critique of the Power of Judgment*, and that title would then have to appear in the text as well. Both follow the existing style. The 1676 visit is stated as standard history and gets no entry unless the text names a source.

- **Decision: Length budgets.** Ch1: +1 paragraph and ≤ 2 sentences. Ch8 §III: ≤ 2 new paragraphs (section ≤ 7). Ch10 §VI: ≤ 1 paragraph.

## Risks / Trade-offs
- **Leibniz becomes a protagonist.** Mitigation: one paragraph in Ch1, introduced as the link between Spinoza and Kant, and used only where Ch8 and Ch10 need him.
- **The participant reads as a seventh component.** Mitigation: Ch8 says so explicitly, and Ch7 is untouched.
- **Participant and projection blur.** Chapter 1 keeps a hierarchy (assembly prior to projections), and monads are substances. Mitigation: the Ch8 passage separates the two words, and verification greps that *participant* is never used for a projection.
- **The three altitudes read as a synthesis that dissolves the Spinoza–Kant tension.** Mitigation: the Ch10 paragraph says the tension is held, not resolved, and names the Spinoza–Leibniz rivalry outright.
- **The five questions overclaim.** Mitigation: they are presented as a proposed form, not a demonstrated one.
- **Ch8 §III grows long.** Mitigation: hard cap of two paragraphs. If the passage needs more, raise it as an open question rather than adding a section.
- **Stack leakage.** Mitigation: a verification grep for implementation names and for "use case".
